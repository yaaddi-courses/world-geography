# Continents and oceans

**Covers:** Continent, Ocean

## The big picture of the planet {#overview}
Before you learn any country, it helps to know the largest pieces of the map. Earth's surface is divided into land and water, and each of those is divided again into named parts. About 71 percent of the surface is water and about 29 percent is land. Nearly every place, event or journey you read about can be placed inside one of these big pieces first, and then located more exactly.

## Continent {#continent}
A continent is one of the seven great landmasses: Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Europe, North America, Oceania and South America. They differ enormously in size. Asia is the largest, with about 44 million square kilometres, while Oceania, the smallest, has around 8.5 million.

Continents are partly a matter of convention. Europe and Asia share one connected landmass, but history and culture have long treated them as two. Some countries and some books count the Americas as one continent, or Oceania as Australia alone. This course uses the seven-continent list above, and it is the one you will meet most often in tests and atlases.

Most people live on a few of them. Asia has well over half of the world's population, and Antarctica has no permanent residents, only researchers who rotate in and out.

## Ocean {#ocean}
An ocean is one of the five huge bodies of salt water that cover most of the Earth: the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic. All five are connected, so some geographers speak of a single World Ocean, but each has its own name, currents and character.

The Pacific is by far the largest. It holds nearly half of the world's ocean water and is bigger than all the land on Earth put together. The Atlantic is the second largest and separates the Americas from Europe and Africa. The Indian Ocean lies mainly in the southern half of the globe and touches Africa, Asia and Australia. The Southern Ocean surrounds Antarctica. The Arctic is the smallest and shallowest, and much of it is covered by sea ice.

Oceans matter beyond maps. They store heat, produce much of the oxygen we breathe, supply food and carry most world trade.

## A worked example {#example}
Place five famous locations by continent and by nearby ocean:

| Place | Continent | Nearby ocean |
|---|---|---|
| Cairo, Egypt | Africa | Atlantic and Indian are both far away; the Mediterranean is closest |
| Rio de Janeiro, Brazil | South America | Atlantic |
| Sydney, Australia | Oceania | Pacific |
| Mumbai, India | Asia | Indian |
| Reykjavik, Iceland | Europe | Atlantic, near the Arctic |

The first row shows that a place does not always touch an ocean directly. Cairo sits near the Mediterranean, a sea connected to the Atlantic. Naming the sea, not forcing an ocean, is the better answer.

A quick check of proportions: if the seven continents together cover about 149 million square kilometres, and the whole surface is about 510 million, then land is about 149 divided by 510, which is 29 percent. That matches the figure at the start.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Mixing up "continent" and "country". A country is a political unit with borders and a government. A continent is a physical landmass that may hold dozens of countries. Another mistake is calling the Southern Ocean "the Antarctic" and forgetting that Antarctica is land, while the Southern Ocean is the water around it.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Earth has seven continents and five oceans.
- About 71 percent of the surface is water, and the Pacific alone is larger than all the land.
- The continent list is a convention, and Europe and Asia share one landmass.
- A continent holds many countries. Do not confuse it with a country.
