# Continents and oceans

**Covers:** Continent, Ocean

## Start with the big pieces {#overview}
When you hear about a faraway place, your mind probably files it somewhere first: "that is in Asia", or "that is near the Atlantic". That habit is exactly how geographers work. They start with the largest pieces of the map and then narrow down. About 71 percent of the Earth's surface is water and about 29 percent is land, and both halves have named parts. Let's learn those parts, because every country you study later fits inside one of them.

## Continent {#continent}
Think of a continent as one of the great blocks of land on the map. We count seven: Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Europe, North America, Oceania and South America. Their sizes differ a lot. Asia is the largest, with about 44 million square kilometres, while Oceania, the smallest, has around 8.5 million.

Here is the part that confuses people at first: the list is a convention, not a law of nature. Europe and Asia sit on one connected landmass, yet history and culture have long treated them as two. Some books count the Americas as a single continent, and some treat Oceania as Australia alone. This course uses the seven-continent list above, the one you will meet most often in tests and atlases.

People are spread unevenly across them. Asia holds well over half of the world's population. Antarctica has no permanent residents, only researchers who come and go.

## Ocean {#ocean}
An ocean is a huge body of salt water. Five of them cover most of the planet: the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic. They are all connected, so some geographers talk about one World Ocean, but each has its own name, currents and character.

The Pacific is by far the largest. It holds nearly half of the world's ocean water and is bigger than all the land on Earth put together. The Atlantic comes second and lies between the Americas on one side and Europe and Africa on the other. The Indian Ocean lies mostly in the southern half of the globe and touches Africa, Asia and Australia. The Southern Ocean surrounds Antarctica. The Arctic is the smallest and shallowest, and much of it is covered by sea ice.

Oceans matter well beyond the map. They store heat, produce much of the oxygen we breathe, supply food and carry most world trade.

## A worked example {#example}
Let's place five well-known locations by continent and by nearby ocean.

| Place | Continent | Nearby ocean |
|---|---|---|
| Cairo, Egypt | Africa | Atlantic and Indian are both far away; the Mediterranean is closest |
| Rio de Janeiro, Brazil | South America | Atlantic |
| Sydney, Australia | Oceania | Pacific |
| Mumbai, India | Asia | Indian |
| Reykjavik, Iceland | Europe | Atlantic, near the Arctic |

Look at the first row. Cairo does not touch an ocean at all. It sits near the Mediterranean, a sea that connects to the Atlantic. When a place has no ocean next to it, name the sea. That is a better answer than forcing an ocean onto the map.

Now a quick check that the land and water figures agree. The seven continents together cover about 149 million square kilometres, and the whole surface of the Earth is about 510 million. Divide 149 by 510 and you get about 0.29, so land is 29 percent. That matches the figure at the start, and the remaining 71 percent is water.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is mixing up "continent" and "country". A country has borders and a government. A continent is a physical landmass that can hold dozens of countries.

A second slip is calling the Southern Ocean "the Antarctic". Antarctica is the land, and the Southern Ocean is the water around it. Keep land and water apart in your head and this one goes away.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Earth has seven continents and five oceans.
- About 71 percent of the surface is water, and the Pacific alone is larger than all the land.
- The list of continents is a convention. Europe and Asia share one landmass.
- A continent holds many countries, so do not confuse it with a country.
