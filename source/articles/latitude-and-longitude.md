# Latitude, longitude and the hemispheres

**Covers:** Equator, Hemisphere, Prime meridian, Latitude, Longitude

## A grid on a ball {#overview}
Suppose you want to tell a friend exactly where a ship is, and you cannot point at a map. You need an address that works anywhere on the sea. People solved this by drawing an imaginary grid over the globe. One set of lines runs around the Earth like rings. The other set runs from pole to pole like the segments of an orange. Every point on Earth sits at one ring and one segment, so it gets two numbers. Those numbers are called coordinates, and they can find any place from a ship, a plane or a phone.

## Equator {#equator}
The equator is the ring around the middle of the Earth, exactly halfway between the North and South Poles. It is about 40,000 kilometres long, and it splits the globe into a northern half and a southern half.

Places near it have warm weather all year, because the sun stays high in the sky. It passes through countries such as Ecuador, Kenya and Indonesia. Ecuador is even named after it.

## Hemisphere {#hemisphere}
A hemisphere is half of the Earth. There are two usual ways to cut the globe. The equator gives the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. The prime meridian, together with the line on the opposite side of the world, gives the Eastern and Western Hemispheres.

So every place belongs to two hemispheres at once. Canada is in the Northern and Western ones, while New Zealand is in the Southern and Eastern ones. The seasons are opposite across the equator: when it is summer in the north, it is winter in the south.

## Prime meridian {#prime-meridian}
The prime meridian is the line that runs from pole to pole through Greenwich, near London, in England. It marks zero degrees of longitude. Countries agreed on it in 1884, so that maps and clocks would share one starting point.

Halfway round the world, on the opposite side, is the 180 degree line. It runs mostly through the Pacific Ocean, and the international date line follows it roughly.

## Latitude {#latitude}
Latitude tells you how far north or south of the equator a place is, in degrees. The equator is 0 degrees, and the poles are 90 degrees north and 90 degrees south. Lines of latitude are also called parallels, because they never meet.

One degree of latitude is about 111 kilometres on the ground, wherever you stand. That gives you a handy way to estimate north-south distances in your head.

## Longitude {#longitude}
Longitude tells you how far east or west of the prime meridian a place is, from 0 to 180 degrees. Lines of longitude are called meridians, and they all meet at the poles. This makes longitude behave differently from latitude: a degree of longitude is about 111 kilometres at the equator, and it shrinks to nothing at the poles.

## A worked example {#example}
Let's read the coordinates of three cities and name their hemispheres.

| City | Latitude | Longitude | Hemispheres |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tokyo | 35.7 degrees N | 139.7 degrees E | Northern, Eastern |
| Nairobi | 1.3 degrees S | 36.8 degrees E | Southern, Eastern |
| Lima | 12.0 degrees S | 77.0 degrees W | Southern, Western |

Now let's estimate a distance. Tokyo is at 35.7 degrees north and Nairobi at 1.3 degrees south. They are on opposite sides of the equator, so we add the two numbers: 35.7 + 1.3 = 37 degrees apart. At about 111 kilometres per degree, that is 37 times 111, which is about 4,100 kilometres. This is only the north-south part of the gap. The real distance is larger, because the two cities also differ in longitude.

One more thing to notice: Nairobi's latitude is only 1.3 degrees south, so it sits very close to the equator. The number tells you that before you open a map.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is mixing up latitude and longitude. A memory aid: lines of latitude look like the rungs of a ladder, going across, and they give the north-south position.

Another slip is dropping the direction letter. "36.8" on its own could be east or west, and those two places are on opposite sides of the world. Always keep the N, S, E or W with the number.

## Key points {#key-points}
- The equator is 0 degrees latitude and splits the globe into the Northern and Southern Hemispheres.
- The prime meridian is 0 degrees longitude, runs through Greenwich and divides east from west.
- Latitude measures north or south, and longitude measures east or west, both in degrees.
- One degree of latitude is about 111 kilometres, but a degree of longitude shrinks toward the poles.
